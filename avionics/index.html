MachWorks avionics: the ICARUS bay frame

On the MachWorks avionics subteam I'm designing the avionics bay frame for ICARUS, the team's Mach 0.3+ test jet. Before that I did a little work on the servo harness (the sections further down are from that), and before joining avionics I ran the camera-tracking project as PM through 2025.

The bay frame

The bay frame is the printed structure in the nose that holds the flight electronics: the Jetson, two IMUs, a barometer, the RFD900x radio, the GPS board, the PCA9685 PWM driver, the RC receiver, the landing gear control box, two batteries and the battery distribution board. It gets printed in polycarbonate on the lab's Bambu printer and attaches to ICARUS with heat-set inserts, with screws going through the skin from the outside. The Jetson's barrel jack and Ethernet port have to stay reachable, the IMUs have to line up with the vehicle's body axes, and their final position relative to the CG gets handed to the software team for the estimator. The space available inside ICARUS isn't set yet (it depends on the recovery bay), so the envelope is still open.

What the harness has to do

The servo harness is the wiring and the servos that move ICARUS's control surfaces. Next to a running jet engine it faces current surges when several surfaces slew at once, electrical noise on the control signal, vibration that works connectors loose, and radiated heat. None of those are interesting on their own — what matters is which one gives out first, and how much margin there is before it does.

Then: will the servos survive a jet?

On the avionics subteam I took on the servo harness — the wiring and servos that move ICARUS's control surfaces. Next to a running engine that harness faces current surges, electrical noise, vibration and heat, and a control surface that goes limp mid-flight is not an option. I worked it as a failure-mode analysis: for each way the harness could fail, a bench test to provoke it and measure the margin.

Failure modeWhat provokes itHow I test it
Brownout / voltage sagSeveral control surfaces slewing at once, drawing peak current togetherAdjustable-load bench; measure current at stall and near-stall on paired servos
PWM degradation from engine EMIElectrical noise from the jet engine corrupting the control signalRun the harness beside the engine, mimic the carbon-fibre shielding, capture PWM on an Analog Discovery, verify visually with a camera
Connector failureAirframe vibration working a connector looseVibration bench to find how much shake unseats a connector
Thermal failureRadiated heat from the engineHeat-gun bench matched to propulsion's measured engine temperatures
Destructive limitCombined stall current + heatPush a servo to failure to find the true margin
Where it stands — the FMEA and the test benches are designed and the first runs (EMI, brownout, torque) are underway; the full end-to-end servo-harness validation is still pending. What's real here is the test method and the initial benches, not a signed-off flight harness.
