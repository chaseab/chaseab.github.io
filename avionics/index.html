MachWorks avionics: designing the camera tracker, then hardening the servo harness

MachWorks is Virginia Tech's high-performance aircraft team; its ICARUS program builds and flies a Mach 0.3+ test jet at the Kentland Experimental Aerial Test Range. I worked the avionics — the electronics that keep the aircraft observable and controllable in flight — in two stages. First, as project manager, I led the preliminary design of the camera-tracking system that keeps a camera pointed at the aircraft through a mission. Then I moved onto the avionics subteam and took on validating the servo harness that drives the control surfaces against the jet's environment. Neither reached a finished flight system — this page is the design and the bench work, told straight.

01 / mission

What the avionics are for

ICARUS is a fast, small test aircraft, and a fast aircraft is only useful if you can see it and trust it during a flight. The avionics I worked on cover both halves of that: a ground system that keeps a camera locked onto the aircraft for post-flight analysis and live feedback, and the servo harness that has to move the control surfaces reliably right next to a running jet engine. The first is a pointing-and-estimation problem; the second is a survive-the-environment problem.

02 / camera tracking

Designed the tracker — as project manager

The camera-tracking system autonomously points a camera at the aircraft throughout a mission: the ground station (or a small module on the aircraft) streams GPS, the system estimates where the aircraft is and will be, and a gimbal slews the camera to keep it in frame. I led the preliminary design as project manager and wrote most of the design report. The architecture:

  • Pointing. A 3-axis DJI Ronin-S gimbal carrying a Fujifilm X-T2 — chosen over a star-tracker in a weighted trade study for slew speed, range of motion, and cost.
  • Link. A Digi XBee 3 PRO telemetry link (~2 km range, <10 ms latency), chosen over LoRa in a second trade study for latency and integration.
  • Estimation. GPS + IMU + magnetometer fused with a Kalman filter, with trajectory prediction to cover processing and link delay, targeting ±1° pointing.
  • Compute + software. A Raspberry Pi 4B running a ROS 2 node stack: telemetry/sensor interface → target-geometry & control → gimbal command over SBUS → an operator dashboard with rosbag logging.
  • External module. A lightweight ESP32-based tracker that can ride on the subject as a second telemetry source.

The report backed those choices with the trade studies, a requirements-and-verification plan (bench slew tests, laser pointing-accuracy test, field GPS test, range test), a risk matrix, and a component + power budget. It is a Preliminary Design Report — a design, not a flown system — and it was a team deliverable: I was PM of four. The cleaned PDR is available on request.

03 / servo harness

Then: will the servos survive a jet?

On the avionics subteam I took on the servo harness — the wiring and servos that move ICARUS's control surfaces. Next to a running engine that harness faces current surges, electrical noise, vibration and heat, and a control surface that goes limp mid-flight is not an option. I worked it as a failure-mode analysis: for each way the harness could fail, a bench test to provoke it and measure the margin.

Failure modeWhat provokes itHow I test it
Brownout / voltage sagSeveral control surfaces slewing at once, drawing peak current togetherAdjustable-load bench; measure current at stall and near-stall on paired servos
PWM degradation from engine EMIElectrical noise from the jet engine corrupting the control signalRun the harness beside the engine, mimic the carbon-fibre shielding, capture PWM on an Analog Discovery, verify visually with a camera
Connector failureAirframe vibration working a connector looseVibration bench to find how much shake unseats a connector
Thermal failureRadiated heat from the engineHeat-gun bench matched to propulsion's measured engine temperatures
Destructive limitCombined stall current + heatPush a servo to failure to find the true margin
Where it stands — the FMEA and the test benches are designed and the first runs (EMI, brownout, torque) are underway; the full end-to-end servo-harness validation is still pending. What's real here is the test method and the initial benches, not a signed-off flight harness.
