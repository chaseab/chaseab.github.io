Project log · started September 2026 · in progress

An SO-101 arm, a dashboard, and a first imitation-learning task

In September 2026 I built a pair of SO-101 arms — a leader I move by hand and a follower that mirrors it — to learn imitation learning on real hardware end to end: wiring and calibration, data collection, training a policy, and running it back on the arm. Everything runs on one Windows desktop with a GTX 1080 Ti. Since 19 Sep most of the training has moved into a MuJoCo simulation of the bench, and the latest policy (trained only on simulated data) grasps and carries blocks on the real arm but doesn't get them into the right spot yet.

Most of the first week went into making the stock LeRobot setup behave on Windows. The things that actually cost time:

  • Serial ports. Bluetooth had claimed COM3–COM6 and Windows gave the two identical CH343 servo boards duplicate names. I pinned each board's port number to its USB serial in the registry; they now survive replugging.
  • Calibration. The leader's shoulder-pan horn is clocked so its mechanical mid-travel sits at encoder count 4092 — two counts under the 4094 ceiling the calibration routine allows — so calibration failed with Magnitude exceeds 2047. Parking that joint off-centre (~3800) before calibrating fixes it and costs no range of motion, since the sweep records the real min/max anyway.
  • Servo bus. Teleop dropped out on bus errors, so I patched a retry into the bus layer and run teleop through a small wrapper instead of the stock command.
  • USB. Both cameras kept dropping off the bus whenever the arm moved. The cause was a USB extension cable on the wrist camera, plus a 4-port hub (Realtek RTS5411) that reset both camera ports whenever one of them glitched. On 14 Sep I moved the extension to the leader's serial link and took the hub out (wrist camera on one root hub, overhead camera and both servo boards on the other), and the dropouts during motion stopped. The server now logs every USB arrival and removal so a bad cable shows up before a recording.

One page to run the rig from

I wanted to see the arm, the cameras, and the training runs in one place, from any device, without a terminal open. The dashboard is a Python server on the desktop and a Three.js page:

  • Live overhead and wrist feeds, with a swap button because Windows re-enumerates the two identical cameras in a different order after any USB disturbance.
  • A virtual twin of the follower driven from the URDF and the servo telemetry (8 Hz), standing on the 3D map from the next section.
  • Calibrate, teleop, record, and train from the browser. The training panel builds the LeRobot record/train commands and validates them by parsing them with LeRobot's own config classes before anything runs.

Mapping the bench from the wrist camera

Goal: while the arm sweeps, build a 3D map of the workspace inside the twin — with no markers, no fiducials, and no dependence on my cutting mat, so the same code works on anyone's SO-101. Camera pose comes from the arm's forward kinematics (FK); geometry comes from a learned depth model rather than per-scene optimisation.

Per keyframe:

  1. undistort
  2. mask the arm out via URDF
  3. MoGe-2 monocular depth
  4. rectify depth to the FK table plane
  5. surfel fusion, best view per region
  6. render as splats

About 1.4 s per frame on the 1080 Ti; one 28-view sweep takes ~90 s and gives a ghost-free scene.

What I measuredResultWhat it changed
FK rotation error0.2 – 0.8°Good enough to use FK as the pose prior.
FK translation error3 – 22 mm, median 8Not good enough on its own; measured against a mat-grid anchor I use only for development.
MoGe-2 support-plane tilt1 – 4°Correcting the pose to that plane made things worse; correcting the depth with a per-pixel ratio field fitted on FK-plane pixels brings on-plane error to 0.2 – 0.8 mm. A plain scale+shift is degenerate on one plane.
View-to-view surface disagreement5 – 25 mmEven with ~1 mm poses, MoGe surfaces disagree between viewpoints — so ICP, guided SIFT PnP, and bird's-eye ECC tracking all failed to beat FK and are switched off. Keeping the best view per region instead of averaging is what removed the ghosting.
Splat size vs. viewer≥ ~2.5 mmBelow that the viewer silently draws nothing at bench distance.
Next — a one-time self-calibration of the ~11 FK parameters (five servo zero-offsets plus a 6-DoF hand-eye transform) from overlapping frames of any textured scene, then live frame-to-model tracking with FK as the prior. Markers stay an optional accuracy add-on, never a requirement.

Teaching it to stack Jenga blocks

The first task I picked for the arm was pick-and-place: build a tower from a pile of loose Jenga blocks, as high as it can reach, and recover when blocks fall. I picked it over the obvious "play Jenga" for concrete reasons:

  • ±5 mm tolerances and a task that is already known to work on this class of arm.
  • Progress is fully visible in the images, so a memoryless reactive policy (ACT) doesn't need to count blocks — it reads the tower top and the pile.
  • Every placement in an episode is a distinct (observation, action) situation, so one full-tower episode is many demonstrations.
  • Recovery is designed in: 2–3 failures are injected per episode and fumbled episodes are never re-recorded, because recovery data is the point.

Pulling blocks out of a real tower is explicitly out of scope for now. Every published robot that has done it used force/torque sensing to tell a loose block from a stuck one, and the STS3215's load register is unlikely to resolve the ~0.3 N that matters. Vision alone can't tell the difference.

The original plan. ACT first, Diffusion Policy second. Training measures 0.40 s/step at batch 8 with two 640×480 cameras on the 1080 Ti — so 30k steps is about 3.3 h. A first checkpoint after 20 six-layer episodes (~360 pick-place cycles); 50–70 episodes total, with one DAgger-style correction round.

Where it started. A 15-minute teleop trial said the task was doable on this rig, and CUDA training was verified on a synthetic dataset. The first real data and the first policy came the next evening — section 05.

Ten demonstrations and a first policy

On the evening of 15 September the loop ran end to end for the first time: teleoperated demonstrations recorded through the dashboard, an ACT policy trained on the 1080 Ti, and the checkpoint driven back on the real arm. The numbers below are from the run logs.

Result. The policy could not grasp a block from the pile. When a block was handed to the gripper, it did carry it to the tower and place it. Motion was jerky. As a stacker: effectively unusable — which is about what ten episodes should buy. The transport-and-place half of the skill exists; the pile grasp doesn't, at ~150 grasp examples over a random heap against the 300+ the plan budgets for it. The jerkiness is ACT executing 2-second action chunks open-loop and switching abruptly between them, made worse by two demo tempos in one dataset (episodes 0–4 careful at 4.5–5.5 min each, 5–9 faster at 2–2.7 min).

What had to be built to get here. Most of the day's work was around the model, not in it:

  • Retry-patched servo bus. robust_record.py and robust_eval.py wrap LeRobot's own entrypoints and monkeypatch the Feetech bus with eight retries per read/write, so a dropped packet no longer ends the episode.
  • VRAM-capped trainer. On Windows the 1080 Ti runs in WDDM mode, where cudaMalloc never fails — it silently pages to system RAM and a 0.4 s step becomes minutes. robust_train.py sets torch.cuda.set_per_process_memory_fraction(0.5) before handing off to lerobot-train; that is the only guard that works there.
  • Camera-dropout resilience. Mid-episode the wrist camera's cable came loose; LeRobot released torque and the arm dropped. Now a camera that drops serves its last good frame and reconnects in the background for up to 20 s, torque is held on any failure, and the recovery park is a self-pacing "leashed" move — the stock park had aborted on following error from a horizontal reach. Verified on the arm: 112° away → home in 22 s.
  • Two smaller ones. A hook that resets a stale sweep speed cap (Goal_Velocity 300 ≈ 26°/s) the moment the follower connects — LeRobot never resets it and it cost the first recording attempt — and a ramp that leashes the follower to the leader's live pose at session start so nothing snaps.

What came after. On 16 Sep I re-ran the same checkpoint with temporal ensembling (n_action_steps=1, temporal_ensemble_coeff=0.01) and with the 3D mapper stopped. The eval loop held about 27 Hz instead of dipping to 7–20 Hz, but the policy still couldn't grasp from the pile, so the problem was the data and not how the chunks were executed. On 17 Sep I recorded a second dataset, intial_stack_v1: 11 episodes (58,197 frames, 32 min) building a 3-layer tower from 9 blocks laid out singly instead of heaped. After that the training moved into simulation (next section). Of the older to-do list, the overhead camera stays straight down, the wrist camera got its own root hub on 14 Sep, and the flat gripper pads were replaced by the gripper redesign below.

Training in simulation

After the real runs I moved the training into simulation: a MuJoCo digital twin of the bench with both calibrated cameras and servo behaviour fitted from a sweep of the real arm (about 120 ms of lag on every joint). The demonstrations come from a scripted expert in the twin (including recovery and correction demos), so none of the training data for these policies came from the real arm.

The first test was a single block: reach it, grasp it, lift it, carry it to a coaster and let go. The first version reached about 8 cm off on the real arm, and it seems the overhead camera was the gap, so the second version was trained with randomised lighting, colours and overhead camera focal length. On 20 Sep that version did the whole sequence 3 out of 3 times on the real arm, although every block was set down about 4 cm from the coaster, which is where the coaster had been in the sim.

The task then changed to picking three Jenga blocks and placing them side by side on the coaster. The timelapse below is one ACT policy (v5) at six points in its training (10k, 50k, 70k, 130k, 160k and 250k steps), each run once on the same scene at 8x speed. It shows the policy learning, it is not a benchmark. On a 10-episode sim evaluation the 250k checkpoint grasped 21 to 23 of 30 blocks and placed 8 to 13 of 30 within 12 mm.

On 24 Sep I ran the 250k checkpoint on the real arm three times. It grasped and carried the first block 3 out of 3 times (the earlier versions had not grasped at all on this task), released it once with the block half on the near edge of the coaster, and got 0 of 9 blocks into their slots. While measuring the bench with a tape I found that the cutting mat's grid is 1/2 inch, not 1 cm, and every calibration had assumed 1 cm, so the model had the overhead camera about 42 cm above the mat instead of about 54 cm and the coaster closer than it is. The overhead camera was recalibrated for the 1/2-inch grid and the simulated data was regenerated on the corrected twin (150 episodes). Training v6 on it, starting from the v5 checkpoint, began on 25 Sep.

Next — evaluate v6 in the twin and then on the real arm. The mat texture in the twin was built from an overhead photo with the arm folded at rest, so every dataset so far has a flat phantom arm lying on the table, and the plan for v7 is to regenerate the data with a cleaned-up, more realistic render. Separately, on 25 Sep I started redesigning the gripper: TPU fin-ray fingers on a quick-change mount that clamps onto the stock jaw, with a PLA fit test before the TPU print. All of the datasets so far use the stock jaws, so those stay restorable.
