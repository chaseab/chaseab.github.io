A $187 sensor platform for oyster water

The Chesapeake's oysters filter the bay, and nobody knows where the good ground is without getting in the water. I built a tethered ROV to carry a sensor package down and report back — five subsystems, on a budget smaller than the donated camera bolted to the front.

01

Why oysters

A single oyster filters sediment, algae, nitrogen and phosphorus out of the water it sits in. That's the whole pitch — they're infrastructure that reproduces itself. The problem is there aren't enough of them left: at the current Chesapeake population, filtering the entire bay once would take over a year.

Plenty of organizations are working to rebuild that population. But unlike agriculture, aquaculture is still rooted in outdated methods with very little modern technology in use. Farmers working bottom culture are making planting and harvesting decisions largely blind — where the hard surfaces are, where sediment would bury spat, which beds have matured.

That gap is what the ROV was aimed at. Cheap enough that a restoration site or a small farm could actually own one, carrying sensors that answer the questions a farmer is otherwise guessing at.

02

What it had to do

The prior year's practicum produced research and a loose design. The capstone was to finalize it, build it, and equip it well enough to be used in a real research setting. Three specifications had to hold for it to count as complete.

SPEC 01 · WATERPROOFING

Every electrical component sealed, including where the tether penetrates the hull. The sample container sealed too — a leak there contaminates the sample rather than shorting a board.

SPEC 02 · DATA COLLECTION

Sensors and telemetry accurate and real-time, without interference. The pressure sensor specifically had to be verified, since depth is derived from it.

SPEC 03 · VISUALIZATION

Data presented intuitively enough to read an environment in real time at the surface, and exportable afterward for analysis.

03

The vehicle

Home-built ROVs converge on PVC for good reasons: it's cheap, it's already watertight, and the voids double as buoyancy. The frame is 1.5in Sch40 — twelve tees, ten elbows — with a port cut in so the frame floods and settles to roughly neutral.

Electronics live in a 4in PVC tube capped at both ends, one cap carrying a 2.5in acrylic dome for the camera's field of view, the other drilled for four tether penetrations. A rubber coupling holds one end on, so the whole electronics stack stays serviceable after assembly instead of being epoxied shut forever.

Leftover 4in stock became motor guards — mounts that let the six bilge-pump thrusters come on and off easily, and keep debris out of the impellers.

Onboard is an Elegoo Mega; topside an Arduino Uno reading a PlayStation 2 controller. Everything between them runs down Cat 6 in a braided sleeve.

04

Getting a signal back up the tether

This was the subsystem that worked best, and the one where the engineering reasoning was cleanest. Radio is useless through water, so the vehicle is tethered — which trades a wireless problem for a signal-integrity one.

Arduino logic swings between 0 and 5 V. Send that down a long conductor and two things eat it: the wire's own resistance drops the voltage, and any passing electromagnetic field induces noise on top. Over a few inches neither matters. Over a tether, a 5 V signal decoded against a 2.5 V threshold does not survive.

RS-485 solves this by refusing to measure against ground at all. It sends the same bit twice, inverted, down a twisted pair — an A wire and a B wire — and the receiver reads only the difference between them. Noise couples into both wires nearly equally, so it cancels in the subtraction. The Arduino still speaks UART, so a transceiver module sits at each end doing the conversion.

The differential trace swings ±5 V against a 0 V threshold and never gets near it. That margin is the entire reason a 5 V microcontroller can talk reliably down a long wet cable.

The same tether carries motor commands the other way. Six bilge pumps act as thrusters, each behind a BTS7960 driver, and speed control is pulse width modulation — the driver switches the 12 V supply on and off fast enough that the motor responds to the average rather than the switching.

Duty cycle is a byte: 0 to 255. That byte, plus a direction bit, is the entire motor command.

05

Sensors, and a sampler that improvised

Temperature and pressure both need to touch the water, which means both need a hull penetration, which means both are leak risks. Rather than accept that risk in the compartment holding the microcontroller, they got their own compartment — a separate 4in PVC section, sensors poking through its end cap. If it floods, it floods alone.

The reference way to sample water at depth is a Nansen or Niskin bottle: lowered open, inverted by a released weight at the target depth, sealed. On this budget, building a mechanism like that which was both Arduino-driven and reliably watertight wasn't realistic.

The workaround used what was already aboard. The thrusters are bilge pumps — so another bilge pump, plumbed to a sealed 4in container with a check valve in the line, becomes a sampler. Press a spare button on the PlayStation controller and it runs.

The container holds about 0.22 gal; the pump moves 1100 GPH. 0.72 seconds should fill it.

THE RELAY THAT WOULDN'T FIRE

The relay switching the sample pump was rated for a 4.5–12 V input. The Mega tops out at 5 V — inside the rated range, so it should have been fine. It wasn't: at 5 V the relay drew enough current to light its own indicator LED and nothing more. The coil never pulled in. The fix was a 2N2222 transistor between the 12 V battery and the relay, letting the Mega switch the real supply with almost no current of its own — a rated range describing voltage while the actual constraint was current.

06

Water gets in through the wires

Every conductor entering the hull is a potential leak path. The technique used throughout is an epoxy well: a short length of ⅛in PVC forms a cup, the wires pass through it, and the cup is filled with epoxy that cures into a solid plug around them.

The non-obvious part — and the detail that makes the difference — is what happens if you pot a bundle with its insulation intact. A nick anywhere along the tether lets water into the jacket, and it will wick inside the insulation, straight through the epoxy plug, and into the compartment. The seal is perfect and the hull still floods.

So the insulation is stripped where the wires cross the epoxy. Bare conductor bonds to the epoxy with no annular channel for water to follow.

07

What actually happened

Subsystems were wet-tested individually. The assembled vehicle never made a piloted dive — so nothing below is a claim about how it swims, surveys or holds station. It's a claim about which parts worked when they got wet.

Telemetry
Worked
RS-485 carried controller input and both sensor channels at 9600 baud, ~40 values/sec, without interference. Best-executed part of the build.
Sensor housing
Worked
The isolated compartment did not leak on its first immersion. The failsafe partitioning was never actually called upon.
Pressure → depth
Miscalibrated
Sensor reads, but depth values are wrong. Never calibrated. Temperature compensation was identified as the fix and never implemented.
Water sampler
Unresolved
Mechanism fired reliably, but the container underfilled — the end caps added volume the 0.72 s calculation didn't account for. The deeper problem is below.
Data dashboard
Worked
Python read the serial stream and plotted it live with Matplotlib. The only real friction was OS security policy blocking COM port access.
Photogrammetry
Failed
Reconstructed a bookshelf in detail on the bench. Produced nothing usable underwater.
Full vehicle dive
Not attempted
The assembled ROV was never operated under power in open water. Thruster response, handling and endurance are unmeasured.
08

The parts that didn't work

Three of these. One is a calibration debt, one is a measurement I accidentally invalidated, and one is a subsystem defeated by the environment it was built for.

Photogrammetry

the biggest failure

The plan: a GoPro Hero 7 shooting on an interval, images fed to RealityCapture afterward to reconstruct the seafloor in 3D. Map oyster beds, plan dredge paths that take mature oysters without tearing up reef and grass.

The pipeline was sound and I proved it. A bench test with a DSLR against a stack of books produced a model detailed enough to read the titles on the covers.

Underwater it produced nothing. Photogrammetry works by finding the same physical point in overlapping photos and triangulating from it. The Chesapeake is turbid — suspended sediment scatters everything, contrast collapses, and the GoPro's very wide angle spreads what detail survives across too few pixels. Close to a feature, the software could not find enough common reference points between frames to solve anything.

Why it counts as a real failure: the turbidity of the Chesapeake was knowable before I chose the approach. I validated the software and the workflow, and never validated the assumption underneath them — that the water would be clear enough to see through. In clearer freshwater this subsystem would probably work as designed. In the water it was built for, it doesn't.

The sampler filtered its own sample

unresolved

The bilge-pump sampler collected water on command, which looked like a win. Then the obvious problem surfaced: a pump is not a neutral conduit. Pulling water through an impeller strains and shears it. Turbidity, suspended solids, anything fragile in the sample — potentially altered in the act of collection.

So the mechanism works and the measurement may not. Whether the samples are scientifically usable is a question for an environmental scientist, and I shelved the project before getting that answer.

The lesson underneath it: I validated that the device did what I built it to do, not that what it produced was worth having. Those are different tests, and only the second one mattered.

Depth readings were never true

calibration debt

The pressure sensor reported cleanly over telemetry. The depth derived from it was wrong, and stayed wrong — it was never calibrated against a known reference.

The fix was identified at the time: water density and therefore the pressure-to-depth relationship shift with temperature, and the ROV was already carrying a temperature sensor in the same housing. The compensation input was available. It was never written.

Straightforwardly unfinished work — not a hard problem, just one I didn't come back to. Depth accuracy was written into spec 02, so that specification was never actually met.

// WHAT I TOOK FROM IT

Three subsystems worked. The one I was proudest of designing is the one that failed.

  • Validate the assumption, not just the pipeline. I proved photogrammetry worked by testing it in clear air on a bookshelf. The variable that actually decided the outcome — how much you can see through Chesapeake water — went untested until it was too late to change approach.
  • "Works" and "produces valid data" are different tests. The sampler filled its container on command and I counted that as success. That the pump was shearing the sample as it collected it is a separate question, and the one that determines whether the subsystem has any value.
  • Rated ranges describe one variable at a time. A relay rated 4.5–12 V that won't close at 5 V isn't mislabelled — the rating was voltage and the binding constraint was current. A 2N2222 and the 12 V rail already onboard solved it in an afternoon.
ON THE FUTURE WORK SECTION

My capstone report closes with plans: calibrate the pressure sensor, expand the sensor package, add machine learning so the vehicle could navigate features well enough to feed the photogrammetry software. I meant all of it at the time. None of it happened. The project has been shelved since 2025 and I don't expect to return to it — stating that plainly here rather than letting the report's ambitions stand in for work I never did.
